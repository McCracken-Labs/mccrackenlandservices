Oklahoma Intestacy Chart

Descent and distribution at a glance · 84 O.S. § 213 · both statutory regimes

This chart shows who inherits Oklahoma property when a person dies without a will. Everything on it comes from one statute, 84 O.S. § 213. Read the box below first if the words are unfamiliar, then find the date of death, then work the table for that date.

Plain words first

Intestate
Dying with no will, or with a will the court refuses to admit. The statute then decides who takes, and the family has no say in it. A will that exists but has never been probated is a separate problem, and the box below covers it.
Probate
The court proceeding that proves a will and moves title under it, or that administers an estate where there is no will. In Oklahoma it is a district court proceeding under Title 58.
Decedent
The person who died.
Issue, meaning descendants
The statute’s word for descendants: children, grandchildren, great-grandchildren, on down. Not spouses, not brothers or sisters, not parents. The statute defines it that way itself at § 213(B)(4), so this chart says descendants everywhere except where it is naming a statutory paragraph.
Kindred
Blood relatives. Marriage does not make someone kindred, which is why the surviving spouse is always handled separately from everyone else on this page.
Collateral relatives
Relatives who are neither ancestors nor descendants. Brothers, sisters, aunts, uncles, nieces, nephews, cousins.
Next of kin in equal degree
The nearest surviving blood relatives, measured by how many steps up and back down the family tree separate them from the person who died. Everyone standing the same number of steps away shares alike.
By right of representation
A dead child's share drops to that child's own children, who split it among themselves. Three children, one of them dead leaving two kids: the two living children take a third each, and the two grandchildren split the last third, a sixth apiece.
Coverture
The period of the marriage. Nothing more than that.
Joint industry
Property the spouses acquired by their joint effort during the marriage. This is an Oklahoma idea and it is not community property. It turns on how the property was acquired, not on whose name is on the deed.
Other property
Everything else the decedent owned. Property brought into the marriage, inherited, or received by gift.
Undivided interest
A share of the whole tract rather than a fenced-off piece of it. Two owners of an undivided half each own half of every acre.
Per capita
By the head. Each person counted takes the same size share, so the fraction depends on how many people there are.
Escheat
The estate goes to the State when nobody qualifies to take it.

Before anything else: a will on file is not a probated will

Executing a will and moving title are two different events, and the gap between them is where this chart gets misused. Oklahoma treats a will as wholly ineffectual to pass title to real property, including minerals and leasehold, until it has been admitted to probate, and a devisee has no rights to enforce under it in the meantime. Filing the will with the county clerk does nothing. A will probated in another state and never probated here does nothing either.

That does not make the will void. It can still be offered for probate, and where an affidavit of death and heirship is used under 16 O.S. § 67, a copy of the unprobated will has to be attached to it. What it means is that a dead record owner with a will in a drawer is a probate problem and not a chart problem. Running the tables below on that estate produces a set of fractions no examining attorney will accept, and the statute is unclear on its own terms whether title in that situation runs to the heirs or to the devisees. Send it to counsel.

Oklahoma Title Examination Standards, comment to 16 O.S. § 67. Attachment requirement at § 67(C).

And a probated will cannot cut the spouse below the joint-industry half

The box above is about a will that was never probated. This one is about a will that was. Even a properly probated will has a floor under it, and the floor is built out of the same joint-industry idea that runs the tables below.

Deaths on or after July 1, 1985. No spouse may devise away from the other so much of the estate that the survivor would receive less in value than an undivided one-half interest in the property acquired by the joint industry of the husband and wife during coverture. A will that gives the survivor less does not fail on its own. The survivor has to claim that half by electing to take it in place of everything the will left them, and the election has to be made affirmatively in writing and filed in the district court on or before the final date for hearing of the petition for final distribution. Miss that date and the will controls.

Deaths before July 1, 1985. The floor was measured differently. No spouse could be devised away from so much that the survivor received less in value than would have been obtained through succession by law, subject to a cap: as to property not acquired by the joint industry of the spouses during coverture, the testator was not required to leave more than one-half in value to the survivor. Same July 1 dividing line as everything else on this page.

Where this surfaces on a runsheet: a will devising everything to the children, a final decree that recites it, and nobody ever checked whether the widow elected. If she did, an undivided half of the joint-industry property never reached the children, and every conveyance out of them since is short by that half. The decree is where the answer is, not the will.

84 O.S. § 44(A) for deaths before July 1, 1985; § 44(B)(1), (2) and (3) for deaths on or after. Both subsections let the homestead be devised by one spouse to the other. This is a testate rule, and it is on this page because the two boxes together fix the outer limits of what any will can do to a spouse.

Start here: find the date of death

Oklahoma changed its intestacy statute effective July 1, 1985, and both versions are still live because both still govern the deaths that happened under them. The trigger is the date of death. Not the date of the deed, not the date probate was opened, not the date the final decree was filed. A 1979 death probated in 1998 is still governed by the old rules.

Ancestral property, and why two paragraphs of the old statute look like nothing else on this page

Seventh and Eighth cover a second death rather than the first one. A child inherits from the decedent, then dies while still under age and never married. Whatever came to that child by inheritance from the decedent does not descend the ordinary way. It goes back across to the other children of the same parent, and to the children of any of those children who are also dead, by right of representation. If every other child of that parent is also dead, it goes to their children. Nothing in the post-1985 statute does this. When an old chain has a minor who died holding an inherited interest, check these two paragraphs before running the ordinary rules.

84 O.S. § 213(A), Seventh and Eighth.

Three places this goes wrong in the field

Each of these has put an interest in the wrong name on a real runsheet.

One. The date of death, not the date of anything else

Probate can open decades after a death, and the decree recites the distribution the court applied at that time. If the death was before July 1, 1985 and someone ran the modern rule, a surviving spouse who should have taken a third is showing a half, and every downstream conveyance carries the error. Get the death certificate date, or the date recited in the decree, before you touch the fractions.

Two. The pre-1985 joint-industry reversion is a second event

Under Second, joint-industry property with no descendants goes wholly to the survivor, and then splits half to each spouse’s heirs when the survivor dies. Chains routinely stop after the first death. If the surviving spouse has since died and nobody ran the reversion, half the interest is standing in the wrong family. Note also that the statute says “the whole estate” in that proviso, and where an old estate mixed joint-industry with other property and left no descendants, the reach of that sentence is a question for counsel rather than a chart. Note too that the reversion reaches only what is left: the statute splits the property “at whose death, if any of the said property remain,” so anything the survivor sold or devised away in the meantime is out of it.

Three. Characterize the property before you split it

Four of the rules on this page give a different answer for joint-industry property than for other property. Joint industry turns on how the property was acquired and not on whose name is on the deed, so the deed alone will not tell you. Minerals bought with marital earnings are usually joint industry even where one spouse took title alone. Minerals inherited, gifted, or owned before the marriage usually are not. Where the record does not show it, that is a fact question, and guessing it is how a half becomes a third.

Facts that change the answer and are not on this chart

Status rules sit on top of the tables above and can move a share or remove a taker entirely. Watch for half brothers and half sisters, adopted children, children born after the decedent’s death, children born outside the marriage, advancements made during the decedent’s life, and heirs who predeceased. Restricted Indian interests follow their own descent rules and are outside 84 O.S. § 213 altogether. Any of these turns a chart lookup into a question for the examining attorney.

Full statute text: 84 O.S. § 213. The definition of “by right of representation” and of “issue” appears at § 213(B)(4) and is written to apply to the section. This chart uses the plain word descendants wherever the statute says issue, because § 213(B)(4) defines them as the same thing. “Joint industry” means property acquired by the joint effort of the spouses during the marriage; it is a rule of descent and not a property right during the marriage.
